AI Roles Poll Results

Understanding Perspectives on Four Possible Roles of AI

Key Takeaway

Main Finding

There is strong consensus that AI as Cognitive Extension represents the most promising direction for educational AI, with 5 out of 7 respondents ranking it first. The key insight: respondents value AI's capacity to enable truly transformational cognitive work that is fundamentally impossible for humans to perform alone—not merely automating existing tasks or replacing human interactions. This role breaks beyond typical EdTech approaches (personalized tutoring, teacher efficiency tools) to open genuinely novel possibilities. AI as Assessor emerged as a notable second choice with 2 first-place votes, revealing a split: some view it as the "heart of capabilities/building block work" that overcomes human limitations in processing large-scale data objectively, while others warn of premature implementation risks and LLM bias. AI as Social Partner consistently ranked lowest, with concerns about replacing human strengths and ethical risks in K-12 settings, though some see potential value if designed specifically as a learning (not social) companion. The overall preference reflects a desire to use AI for augmentation and impossibility-breaking rather than substitution or convenience.

Role Rankings & Feedback Summary

1

AI as Cognitive Extension

AI becomes part of the cognitive system itself, enabling cognition that would be computationally impossible for humans alone—processing at scales beyond human capability, maintaining complex representations, performing rapid iterations, and integrating across domains.

Why people ranked this here:
  • Most transformational role: Enables truly novel cognitive work that humans cannot do alone, not just automating existing tasks
  • High pedagogical impact: Addresses student engagement, supports personalized projects, helps teachers process diverse student work
  • Feasible with current technology: LLM APIs make this highly achievable now (wasn't possible 3 years ago)
  • Breaks beyond typical EdTech: Goes beyond personalized tutoring or teacher efficiency tools to open new possibilities
  • Equity potential: Supports varied learning contexts, multiple design variations, and diverse knowledge bases
2

AI as Assessor

AI observes, measures, and provides feedback—offering measurable progress on skill development, monitoring how students respond to feedback over time, and tracking how they integrate new information into their thinking.

Why people ranked this here:
  • Strong advocacy from some: "Heart of capabilities/building block work" that should be central to all co-design; addresses critical teacher pain points for durable skills assessment
  • Overcomes human limitations: Helps process large amounts of data per student, handle multiple formats, and evaluate work more objectively—scores highest on equity criteria
  • Foundational for future work: Will support all future cohort tools and provide essential building blocks
  • Split opinion with major concerns: Risk of premature implementation causing harm; LLM bias toward standardized skill demonstrations; requires deep research, data collection, and bias checking; needs groundwork on equitable assessment practices; human validation coding is slow; should be developed carefully before models are released
3

AI as Environment/Context

AI creates immersive spaces where students can practice skills or learn—offering simulated environments, providing situational contexts, creating exploratory spaces for testing ideas, and enabling "what if" scenarios.

Why people ranked this here:
  • Strong pedagogical value: Enables practice of durable skills in simulated real-world environments
  • Complementary to cognitive extension: Related goals of supporting transformational learning
  • Particularly effective for certain domains: Strong potential for science/virtual experiments
  • Concerns: May require intensive multimodal/VR development; accessibility challenges across different bodies and needs; concept clarity issues; may not translate equally across all subject areas; doesn't fit well with authentic, place-based learning approaches; risk of stereotypes in adaptive environments
4

AI as Social Partner

AI engages in back-and-forth dialogue with learners, acting as an interactive conversation partner—asking clarifying questions to inspire reflection, responding in natural language, adapting communication style, and facilitating discussions.

Why people ranked this here:
  • Ethical concerns with children: Parallels to social media and AI companion risks; still raw and unexplored in K-12 settings; concerns about young people turning to AI as social companions
  • Risk of replacing human strengths: Could promote excessive screen time and diminish human interactions
  • May limit creative thinking: Could default to "helper agent" approaches rather than innovative designs
  • Potential positives with caveats: Real-time feedback that teachers can't provide; small group work support is interesting but challenging; communication style adaptation needs depth to avoid stereotypes; could have value if designed specifically as learning companion (not social companion); requires extensive ethical conversations before implementation
